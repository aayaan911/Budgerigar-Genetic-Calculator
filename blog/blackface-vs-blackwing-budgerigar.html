Blackface vs Blackwing Budgerigar, Complete Diagnostic Guide

Two of the darkest documented budgerigar mutations often get grouped together as one thing on breeder forums, but they are separate mutations at separate genetic loci with different visible effects. Blackface was described in the Netherlands in 1992 and darkens the face mask, forehead, and body. Blackwing was described in Venezuela in 2002 and darkens the wing flight feathers to solid black. Both are autosomal recessive. Both combine to produce Double Black, the darkest budgerigar phenotype currently possible. This guide covers nine visible identification markers, the inheritance mechanics, the Double Black breeding path, and 2026 market pricing.

TL;DR

Blackface darkens the face mask, forehead, and body while leaving wings mostly normal. Blackwing darkens the wing flight feathers to solid black while leaving the face mostly normal. Both are autosomal recessive, both are non-allelic (different loci), and both require two copies for the visible phenotype. A bird with only Blackface looks dark-faced with standard wings. A bird with only Blackwing looks standard-faced with black wings. A Double Black bird has two copies of each and shows both dark face and black wings simultaneously. The Double Black combination requires four generations of controlled breeding starting from split-carrying stock.

Why these two mutations get confused

Both Blackface and Blackwing are melanistic budgerigar mutations, both are autosomal recessive, both produce visibly darker birds than any standard mutation, and both are rare enough that most breeders have seen photos more often than the birds themselves. In my aviary experience, first-time buyers confuse them constantly when browsing exhibition photos, because the word black in both mutation names implies the same effect.

The visible effects are actually opposite in location. Blackface darkens the front of the bird (mask, forehead, throat, body wash). Blackwing darkens the sides of the bird (flight feathers, secondaries). A bird with only Blackface has a dark face and normal wings. A bird with only Blackwing has a normal face and solid black wings.

The confusion gets worse in Double Black birds (which carry both mutations at homozygous status). Double Black shows both dark face AND black wings, which reads visually as one uniformly dark bird rather than two overlapping mutations. Photos of Double Black on Facebook and TikTok are often mislabeled as just Blackface or just Blackwing because casual observers cannot separate the visible components.

According to Terry Martin's 2002 monograph, Blackface and Blackwing sit at different loci and inherit independently, which is why their combined homozygous state (Double Black) produces the darkest possible phenotype. Neither mutation alone approaches the darkness of the combined form.

The nine visible identification markers

The most reliable single marker is the wing flight feathers. Blackwing produces solid black or near-black flight feathers with sharp contrast against the body. Blackface leaves flight feathers only slightly darkened. If the wings are dramatically black, the bird has Blackwing (possibly plus Blackface if Double Black).

The second most reliable marker is the face mask. Blackface heavily darkens the mask (yellow becomes brown-yellow or dark, white becomes grey or dark). Blackwing leaves the mask essentially normal. If the mask is clearly dark, the bird has Blackface (possibly plus Blackwing if Double Black).

The test pairing that separates the two mutations

One test pairing separates a Blackface bird from a Blackwing bird when visual identification is ambiguous. Pair the suspect bird to a confirmed visible Blackface bird from a documented breeder.

If the suspect bird is Blackface: The chicks will include roughly 100 percent visible Blackface offspring, because both parents carry two Blackface alleles at the same locus. Every chick inherits one Blackface allele from each parent, ending up homozygous for Blackface (visible).

If the suspect bird is Blackwing: The chicks will all look Normal (not Blackface, not Blackwing). This is because the Blackwing parent contributes no Blackface alleles, and the confirmed Blackface parent contributes one Blackface allele to each chick. Every chick becomes split for Blackface but visible for nothing. The Blackwing gene from the suspect parent goes to 100 percent of chicks as a Blackwing split, but Blackwing is recessive so a single copy shows no visible effect.

If the suspect bird is Double Black (Blackface plus Blackwing): The chicks will include visible Blackface offspring plus splits for Blackwing. Because the suspect parent contributes both a Blackface allele and a Blackwing allele to every chick, and the Blackface parent contributes a Blackface allele, every chick is visible Blackface plus split for Blackwing. Wait for the first molt at three months to confirm the wing pattern before declaring.

The test is definitive because same-locus recessive mutations always produce visible offspring when paired to each other, while different-locus recessive mutations always produce Normal-looking splits when paired. Terry Martin (2002) codifies this rule as the foundation of budgerigar allele-locus identification.

Inheritance comparison, both recessive but at different loci

Both Blackface and Blackwing are autosomal recessive, meaning two copies of the mutant allele are required to produce the visible phenotype. One copy produces no visible effect (split state, bird looks Normal but carries the gene hidden).

Blackface sits at its own autosomal locus, separate from every other budgerigar mutation. Written in genotype notation, a Blackface bird can be Normal (bf+/bf+, no Blackface alleles), split (bf+/bf, one Blackface allele, Normal-looking), or visual (bf/bf, two Blackface alleles, visibly Blackface).

Blackwing sits at its own separate autosomal locus, non-allelic to Blackface. Written in genotype notation, a Blackwing bird can be Normal (bw+/bw+, no Blackwing alleles), split (bw+/bw, one Blackwing allele, Normal-looking), or visual (bw/bw, two Blackwing alleles, visibly Blackwing).

The critical practical consequence is that Blackface and Blackwing inherit independently. A bird can carry both mutations at various combinations: visible Blackface plus visible Blackwing (Double Black), visible Blackface plus split Blackwing, split Blackface plus visible Blackwing, or split for both mutations (looks Normal, hides both). The Blackface Mutation Guide and Blackwing Mutation Guide cover each locus in full depth.

Because both mutations require two copies to show, breeding programs move slowly. Visible Blackface only shows in 25 percent of offspring from split by split pairings, and same for Blackwing. Combining both requires multi-generation breeding, covered in the next section.

The Double Black combination, 4-generation protocol

Double Black is the combination of visible Blackface and visible Blackwing in the same bird. Because both mutations are recessive, the bird must carry two copies of each mutation at each locus. A four-generation breeding protocol is required to reach Double Black starting from split-carrying stock.

Generation 1: Pair a visible Blackface bird with a visible Blackwing bird. Every chick is split for both Blackface and Blackwing but visibly Normal.

Generation 2: Pair two double-split siblings from Generation 1. Offspring distribution is roughly: 6.25 percent visible Blackface visible Blackwing (Double Black candidates), 12.5 percent visible Blackface split Blackwing, 12.5 percent split Blackface visible Blackwing, 56.25 percent split for one or both mutations, 12.5 percent Normal.

Generation 3: Test-breed the Double Black candidates to confirm both mutations are homozygous. Pair the candidate to a Normal Light Green bird. If offspring are all Normal-looking splits, the candidate is confirmed Double Black.

Generation 4: Pair confirmed Double Black birds together. Every chick is Double Black, producing a stable Double Black breeding line.

The Double Black Breeding Guide covers this protocol in full depth including feed schedules, calcium supplementation for the longer breeding cycles, and the WBO judging criteria for Double Black show birds.

Double Black is currently the darkest budgerigar phenotype possible. As of 2026, Blackwing plus Anthracite plus Blackface combinations are being explored to produce potentially darker birds, but no confirmed Triple mutation birds have been publicly documented.

2026 rarity and market prices

Both mutations exist worldwide in small numbers, but Blackface is slightly more common than Blackwing.

Blackface original Dutch stock from 1992 spread through European exhibition circuits, then reached the UK and US by the early 2000s. Australian breeders imported Blackface in the 2010s. Modern populations are stable in Germany, Netherlands, UK, Italy, US, and Australia. Estimated worldwide population is 3,000 to 5,000 birds as of 2026, mostly at split status because visible Blackface is difficult to maintain in numbers.

Blackwing was first documented in Venezuela in 2002 and spread through Latin American exhibition circuits before reaching Europe in the mid-2010s. Modern Blackwing populations are stable in Venezuela, Brazil, Spain, Germany, and small numbers in the UK and Australia. Estimated worldwide population is 2,000 to 3,500 birds as of 2026.

Which mutation should you breed first?

For most breeders starting a melanistic budgerigar program, Blackface is the more accessible starting point. Blackface stock is more widely distributed globally, breeding stock costs slightly more but is easier to source, and split Blackface birds are widely available from documented breeders in Europe and North America.

Blackwing is worth pursuing if you already have Blackface established or if you specifically want to build toward Double Black as a program goal. Blackwing alone produces striking exhibition birds with dramatic wing contrast, but the population is smaller and imports are harder to source outside Latin America and Spain.

For breeders serious about Double Black, the fastest path is parallel acquisition: buy one visible Blackface and one visible Blackwing from different documented breeders and start the four-generation protocol immediately. This costs USD 3,000 to USD 6,000 upfront but reaches Double Black in about three years of controlled breeding. Buying two Double Black birds directly costs USD 12,000 to USD 30,000+ and skips the multi-generation build.

Whichever mutation you choose, keep detailed pedigrees, use the Budgerigar Genetics Calculator to plan every pairing including split-status tracking, and test breed suspect birds before including them in your main breeding program. Rare recessive mutations reward disciplined record keeping more than any other mutation category.

Frequently asked questions about blackface vs blackwing

What is the difference between Blackface and Blackwing budgerigars?

Blackface darkens the face mask, forehead, and body but leaves wings mostly normal. Blackwing darkens the wing flight feathers to solid black but leaves the face mostly normal. Both are autosomal recessive mutations at separate loci (non-allelic), inheriting independently. When combined in one bird, they produce Double Black, the darkest documented budgerigar phenotype. A bird with only Blackface has dark face and standard wings. A bird with only Blackwing has standard face and solid black wings.

Are Blackface and Blackwing the same mutation?

No. They are separate mutations at separate genetic loci. Blackface was described in the Netherlands in 1992 at its own autosomal recessive locus. Blackwing was described in Venezuela in 2002 at a different autosomal recessive locus. Because the loci are different (non-allelic), the two mutations inherit independently and can combine in the same bird to produce Double Black, which shows both dark face and solid black wings simultaneously.

How do I test if my bird is Blackface or Blackwing?

Pair the suspect bird to a confirmed visible Blackface bird from a documented breeder. If offspring are all visible Blackface, the suspect is Blackface (same locus, both alleles). If offspring are all Normal-looking (split for Blackface), the suspect is Blackwing (different locus). If offspring are visible Blackface plus split Blackwing, the suspect is Double Black. This test settles the identification in one breeding cycle. Wait for the first molt at three months to confirm wing patterns.

What is Double Black budgerigar?

Double Black is a budgerigar carrying two copies of Blackface and two copies of Blackwing simultaneously. It shows both dark face mask and solid black wing flight feathers, producing the darkest documented budgerigar phenotype. Because both mutations are recessive at separate loci, producing Double Black requires four generations of controlled breeding starting from stock split for both mutations. Prices reach USD 6,000 to USD 15,000 or more for confirmed Double Black birds.

Which is more expensive, Blackface or Blackwing?

Blackface is slightly more expensive due to wider global distribution and established exhibition status. Visible Blackface sells for USD 1,500 to USD 3,000 (standard) or USD 3,000 to USD 5,000+ (show quality). Visible Blackwing sells for USD 1,200 to USD 2,500 (standard) or USD 2,500 to USD 4,500+ (show quality). Confirmed splits sell for USD 350 to USD 800 depending on documentation quality. Double Black birds command premium prices of USD 6,000 to USD 15,000 or more.
