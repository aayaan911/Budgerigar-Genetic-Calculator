Sex-Linked Genetics Explained

In budgerigars, Ino, Opaline, Cinnamon, and Slate are sex-linked recessive (SL) — they behave differently depending on whether the carrier is a cock or hen. Hens cannot be splits — they either show the mutation visually or don't carry it at all. Cocks can carry the gene without showing it. This calculator handles sex-linked inheritance automatically, showing cocks and hens in separate columns.

Dark Factor Inheritance

Dark factor is incompletely dominant. Each copy visibly darkens the body color. Zero copies = Light Green / Sky Blue. One copy = Dark Green / Cobalt. Two copies = DD Green / Mauve. Pairing two Cobalt birds gives 25% Sky Blue, 50% Cobalt, 25% Mauve offspring — the classic 1:2:1 Mendelian ratio for incomplete dominance. This is why dark factor is one of the easiest mutations to predict and control in budgerigars.

Grey, Violet & Yellow Face Modifiers

Grey, Violet, and Yellow Face are autosomal incompletely dominant modifiers — each can be Single Factor (SF, one copy) or Double Factor (DF, two copies). Grey factor turns the body grey on blue series or grey-green on green series. Violet adds a violet sheen most visible on cobalt blue (the famous Visual Violet). Yellow Face adds yellow pigment to the face on blue-series birds. The calculator tracks all combinations independently.

What does "split" mean in budgerigar genetics?

A "split" bird visually appears normal but carries one copy of a recessive mutation gene. When paired with another split or a visual mutation bird, they can produce mutation offspring. The calculator shows all splits in the predicted offspring with their exact probability.

How do I know if a mutation is sex-linked in budgies?

In budgerigars, Ino, Opaline, Cinnamon, and Slate are sex-linked recessive (SL). For sex-linked mutations, hens cannot be splits — they either show the mutation visually or don't carry it at all. The calculator automatically separates cock and hen offspring predictions for SL traits.

Why does the calculator embed the dark factor into the base color name?

Because the dark factor is a separate gene from the blue locus, and embedding it into the base color name matches how budgie breeders actually think and speak. A "Cobalt" is genetically bb at the blue locus + Dd at the dark factor locus (heterozygous, one dark factor). A "Mauve" is bb + DD (homozygous, two dark factors). By baking dark factor into the base name (Sky Blue / Cobalt / Mauve, Light Green / Dark Green / DD Green), the user picks one option and the engine handles both gene loci correctly. Two Cobalts paired produces 25% Sky, 50% Cobalt, 25% Mauve — the 1:2:1 incomplete-dominance ratio.

What is the difference between Single Factor (SF) and Double Factor (DF)?

For incompletely dominant mutations like Spangle, Grey, Violet, Yellow Face, and Dominant Pied — Single Factor means one copy of the mutation gene (heterozygous), Double Factor means two copies (homozygous). SF and DF often look visually different. A DF Spangle is nearly all yellow (green base) or white (blue base) and is often confused with Lutino/Albino. A DF Grey is slightly more saturated than SF Grey. A DF Yellow Face shows stronger yellow expression than SF. The calculator outputs SF and DF separately so breeders can plan exhibition pairings precisely.

How does Visual Violet work — why isn't it a base color?

Visual Violet is not a separate gene — it's a Cobalt blue (one dark factor) carrying the Violet factor (Vv or VV). In the calculator, you select Cobalt as the base color and then add Violet Factor as a mutation. The result is named "Visual Violet" automatically. Adding Violet to a Sky Blue gives Violet Sky, adding it to Mauve gives Violet Mauve — but the most striking visual violet effect requires the cobalt dark factor combination. This is documented in Terry Martin's 2002 reference.

What is the difference between Recessive Pied and Dominant Pied in budgies?

They are two completely separate genes with different inheritance modes. Recessive Pied (also called Danish Pied or Harlequin) is autosomal recessive — both parents must carry it for chicks to show it visually. It typically produces large irregular yellow or white patches over the body. Dominant Pied (also called Banded Pied or Australian Pied) is autosomal incompletely dominant — a single factor (SF) bird shows a clean band of pied across the chest, while a double factor (DF) bird shows much more pied area. The two pied types can be combined in the same bird and the result is sometimes called "Double Pied" or "Dark-eyed Clear" depending on whether Ino is also involved.

What is Lacewing and why isn't it in the calculator?

Lacewing is a sex-linked combination mutation — it is actually a budgerigar showing both Ino and Cinnamon at the same time (both sex-linked recessives on the Z chromosome). True genetic Lacewing requires specific Z-chromosome configurations and is rare. To predict Lacewing offspring, set up parents with both Ino and Cinnamon visual or split, and the calculator will show the combinations that would visually express as Lacewing. The "Lacewing" name itself isn't a separate gene — it's a phenotype of the Ino+Cinnamon combination.

Why is Ino sex-linked in budgies but autosomal in lovebirds?

This is one of the most important differences between budgie and lovebird genetics. In budgerigars, the Ino gene sits on the Z sex chromosome — making it sex-linked recessive, so hens can be visual Ino but never split. In peach-faced lovebirds, the Ino gene sits on an autosome — making it autosomal recessive, so both sexes can be split. The visual result (full melanin removal, red eyes) is similar, but the inheritance pattern is fundamentally different. Calculators built for one species cannot be reliably used for the other.

How to Read Your Results

Understanding the Percentages

Each result shows a probability — not a guarantee. These are Mendelian inheritance percentages across a statistically large number of chicks. In any single clutch of 4–6 eggs, the actual outcome will vary.

What "25% Split Cinnamon" Means

If a result shows 25% Split Cinnamon, it means that on average, 1 in every 4 chicks from this pairing will carry the Cinnamon gene without showing it visually. In a single clutch of 4-6 eggs you might get 0, 1, or 2 split chicks — the 25% is a long-run average, not a per-clutch promise. Over many pairings and clutches, the ratio will converge toward 25%.

Cock and Hen Columns

When a sex-linked mutation like Ino, Opaline, Cinnamon, or Slate is involved, the calculator splits results into a cock column and a hen column. This is because sex-linked recessive genes behave differently by sex — hens cannot be splits. Any percentage shown under the hen column for an SL mutation means those chicks will be visual, not split.

Visual vs Split vs SF/DF

Visual means the chick shows the mutation in its feathers. Split means it carries one copy of a recessive gene but looks normal — it can pass the mutation to its own offspring. For incompletely dominant mutations like Dark Factor, Grey, Violet, Spangle, and Yellow Face, Single Factor (SF) and Double Factor (DF) look visually different — DF is typically more saturated or expressed than SF.

Combined Mutations in Results

When both parents carry multiple mutations, the results show every possible combination. A result like "12.5% Cinnamon Cobalt / Opaline" means a chick that visually shows Cinnamon on a Cobalt base and also carries the Opaline gene hidden. These combined outcomes are calculated using independent assortment — each gene pair is calculated separately and then multiplied together for the final probability.
