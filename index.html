Sex-Linked Genetics Explained

In budgerigars, Ino, Opaline, Cinnamon, Slate, Texas Clearbody, and Lacewing are sex-linked recessive (SL), they behave differently depending on whether the carrier is a cock or hen. Hens cannot be splits, they either show the mutation visually or don't carry it at all. Cocks can carry the gene without showing it. This calculator handles sex-linked inheritance automatically, showing cocks and hens in separate columns.

Dark Factor Inheritance

Dark factor is incompletely dominant. Each copy visibly darkens the body color. Zero copies = Light Green / Sky Blue. One copy = Dark Green / Cobalt. Two copies = Olive Green / Mauve. Pairing two Cobalt birds gives 25% Sky Blue, 50% Cobalt, 25% Mauve offspring, the classic 1:2:1 Mendelian ratio for incomplete dominance. This is why dark factor is one of the easiest mutations to predict and control in budgerigars.

Grey, Violet & Yellow Face Modifiers

Grey, Violet, and Yellow Face are autosomal incompletely dominant modifiers, each can be Single Factor (SF, one copy) or Double Factor (DF, two copies). Grey factor turns the body grey on blue series or grey-green on green series. Violet adds a violet sheen most visible on cobalt blue (historically called Visual Violet, now uniformly labelled Violet Cobalt). Yellow Face adds yellow pigment to the face on blue-series birds. The calculator tracks all combinations independently.

Key Inheritance Modes in Budgerigar Genetics

Budgerigar mutations follow five main inheritance modes. The calculator handles all of them correctly with proper Punnett square modelling, allelic series support, and sex-chromosome rules:

  • Autosomal Recessive (AR): Both parents must carry or visually show the gene for chicks to show it (e.g., Recessive Pied, German Fallow, English Fallow, Scottish Fallow, Black Face, Blackwing, Saddleback, Dilute, Greywing, Clearwing).
  • Autosomal Incompletely Dominant (AID): One copy shows partial expression (Single Factor, SF), two copies show full expression (Double Factor, DF). Examples: Dark Factor, Grey, Violet, Yellow Face, Spangle, Dominant Pied, Clearflight Pied, Anthracite, Easley Clearbody.
  • Autosomal Dominant (AD): One parent showing the mutation is enough for chicks to inherit it. Currently the only fully dominant budgerigar mutation: Manto Negro (Brazilian Black Mantle, developed by Ley H. Silva Filho in 2021).
  • Sex-Linked Recessive (SLR): Carried on the Z chromosome (e.g., Opaline, Cinnamon, Lutino/Ino/Albino, Lacewing, Slate, Texas Clearbody). Hens (ZW) cannot be "split" for sex-linked traits, they either show them visually or they do not. Cocks (ZZ) can carry one copy hidden.
  • Allelic Series: Multiple alleles on the same gene locus interact with co-dominance rules. Two main allelic series exist in budgerigars: the dil-locus (Clearwing, Greywing, Dilute) and the ino-locus (Ino, Texas Clearbody). Clearwing × Greywing produces the Fullbody Greywing hetero-allelic phenotype.

About Melopsittacus undulatus, the Budgerigar

Species Overview. The budgerigar (Melopsittacus undulatus) is a small Australian parakeet measuring approximately 18 cm from beak to tail. It is native to the arid interior of Australia and is the only species in the genus Melopsittacus. Wild budgerigars are uniformly Light Green with yellow face and black wing markings. The hundreds of colour mutations bred today, including every mutation modelled in this calculator, have all been developed in captivity since the mid-1800s.

Popularity in Aviculture. The budgerigar is the third most popular pet bird worldwide after canaries and cockatiels, with an estimated 5+ million pet budgies in households globally. It is the most internationally exhibited parrot species, with national championships run by clubs affiliated with the World Budgerigar Organisation (WBO) on every inhabited continent. The Bangladesh Budgerigar Society (BSB), American Budgerigar Society (ABS), and Budgerigar Society of the United Kingdom (BS) are among the largest national bodies.

Lifespan and Breeding. Pet budgerigars average 5 to 10 years of life. With careful diet and stress management, individual birds have lived past 15 years. Sexual maturity arrives at 4 to 6 months but most aviculturists wait until the bird is 8 to 12 months before first breeding for stronger fertility and parental behaviour. Clutch size is 4 to 8 eggs. Incubation is 18 days. Chicks fledge at 30 to 35 days and become independent at 6 to 8 weeks. Hens lay roughly every other day until the clutch is complete.

Budgerigar Genetics Research Timeline, 1875 to 2026

The budgerigar mutation discovery record spans 150 years across four continents. Each entry below represents a stable mutation line that has been verifiably documented and bred true. Sources: Cyril H. Rogers, Dr. Terry Martin (2002), Inte Onsman MUTAVI Research, and the WBO international standards.

  • 1875 — Greywing first documented, Belgium. The earliest stable budgerigar colour mutation on record.
  • 1929 — German Fallow established by Schrapel, Germany. The oldest of the three canonical Fallow lines.
  • 1932 — Recessive Pied (Harlequin) appeared, Denmark.
  • 1933 — Opaline first documented, Australia. The first sex-linked recessive mutation.
  • 1935 — Dominant Pied (Banded Pied) established, Australia.
  • 1937 — English Fallow established, England.
  • 1947 — Scottish Fallow established, Scotland. The third and rarest canonical Fallow line.
  • 1948 — Clearflight Pied (Continental Pied) appeared, Belgium and Netherlands.
  • 1948 — Lacewing first documented in UK aviculture.
  • 1950s — Slate (UK), Texas Clearbody (USA), Cinnamon and Ino formal classification.
  • 1978 — Spangle developed by Melville, Australia.
  • 1992 — Easley Clearbody developed by Steve Easley, California USA.
  • 1992 — Black Face appeared, Netherlands.
  • 1998 — Anthracite established, Germany.
  • 2002 — Blackwing first documented, Venezuela.
  • 2007 — Inte Onsman publishes the Lacewing crossover model at MUTAVI Research, demonstrating Lacewing is a Z-chromosome crossover combination of Cinnamon plus Ino.
  • 2019 — Blackwing arrives in UK via Don Dickson, Edinburgh (16 pure birds and 19 splits in first season).
  • 2021 — Manto Negro (Brazilian Black Mantle) developed by Ley H. Silva Filho, Brazil. The first fully autosomal dominant budgerigar mutation.
  • 2025 — Manto Negro wins Best in Show at the SOCO Cup, Sociedade Ornitológica do Centro Oeste, Brazil. The newest melanistic mutation reaches international competitive exhibition status.

Note. Dates listed are the earliest reliably documented stable lines. Earlier or contemporaneous unstable variants may have appeared without being preserved through verifiable breeding records.

Mutations Not Yet Modelled in This Calculator

Honest transparency matters more than completeness. Several budgerigar phenotypes are deliberately excluded from this calculator until their inheritance pattern can be reliably verified by established references and breeder testing. Listing what we do not model is as important as listing what we do.

  • Faded — Extremely rare globally with limited documented pedigree records. Insufficient verified breeding data to model reliably without producing false offspring predictions.
  • Pre-1875 historical phenotypes — Several pied and dilute-like variants are mentioned in 19th-century English and Belgian aviculture literature but disappeared before stable breeding lines could be established. Without modern test breeding, the inheritance pattern cannot be verified.
  • Single-breeder claims without peer confirmation — Mutations claimed by one breeder without independent reproduction by other established aviculturists are not included until at least two unrelated lines have been verified.
  • Hybrid and cross-species birds — This calculator strictly models pure Melopsittacus undulatus genetics. Crosses with cockatiels, lovebirds, or other Australian parakeets follow different inheritance rules and are intentionally outside scope.
  • Disputed allelic relationships — Where the scientific literature disagrees about whether two phenotypes are allelic or at separate loci, we follow the most recent peer-reviewed reference (typically MUTAVI Research) rather than picking one position arbitrarily.

If you are working with a mutation not modelled here and you have verifiable pedigree records spanning at least three generations, we welcome contact through the KinBird Aviary Facebook page to discuss inclusion in future calculator updates.

Basic Budgerigar Species Facts (Frequently Asked)

How long do budgerigars live?

Budgerigars typically live 5 to 10 years in captivity. With careful diet, low-stress housing, and routine health monitoring, individual birds have lived past 15 years, with documented exceptional cases reaching 18 years. Wild budgerigars rarely exceed 4 to 6 years due to predation, droughts, and food scarcity.

How many eggs do budgerigars lay per clutch?

A budgerigar hen lays 4 to 8 eggs per clutch, with 5 or 6 being the most common count. Eggs are laid every other day until the clutch is complete. Incubation is 18 days from the second egg onwards. Chicks fledge at 30 to 35 days and become independent at 6 to 8 weeks.

What is the scientific name of the budgerigar?

The budgerigar is classified scientifically as Melopsittacus undulatus, the only species in the genus Melopsittacus. It belongs to the family Psittaculidae (Old World parrots). The species was first described by ornithologist George Shaw in 1805.

How many colour mutations do budgerigars have?

This calculator models 24 documented budgerigar colour mutations plus the Dark Factor base-colour gene. Across the global aviculture literature, more than 30 mutation variants have been documented since 1875, including allelic variants and emergent phenotypes like Fullbody Greywing, Dark-Eyed Clear, and Double Black. The 24 modelled here are the verifiable, breed-true mutations with stable inheritance patterns.

Where do budgerigars come from?

Budgerigars are native to the arid interior of Australia, ranging across most of the continent except the densely forested coastal regions. Wild budgerigars travel in large flocks of hundreds to thousands of birds, following rain and food availability. Captive budgerigars are direct descendants of birds first exported to Europe in the 1840s and have been bred for colour mutations continuously since the 1870s.

Budgerigar vs Cockatiel — what is the difference?

Budgerigars (Melopsittacus undulatus) and cockatiels (Nymphicus hollandicus) are both small Australian parrots commonly kept as pets, but they are distinct species in different genera and even different families. Budgerigars are smaller (18 cm vs 32 cm for cockatiels), have shorter lifespans (5-10 vs 15-20 years), produce smaller clutches (4-8 vs 4-6), and follow entirely different mutation genetics. Notably, the Ino gene in budgerigars is sex-linked (on the Z chromosome) while the Ino gene in cockatiels is autosomal recessive. This calculator strictly models budgerigar genetics and should not be used for cockatiel pairings.

What does "split" mean in budgerigar genetics?

A "split" bird visually appears normal but carries one copy of a recessive mutation gene. When paired with another split or a visual mutation bird, they can produce mutation offspring. The calculator shows all splits in the predicted offspring with their exact probability.

Why does the calculator embed the dark factor into the base color name?

Because the dark factor is a separate gene from the blue locus, and embedding it into the base color name matches how budgie breeders actually think and speak. A "Cobalt" is genetically bb at the blue locus + Dd at the dark factor locus (heterozygous, one dark factor). A "Mauve" is bb + DD (homozygous, two dark factors). By baking dark factor into the base name (Sky Blue / Cobalt / Mauve, Light Green / Dark Green / Olive Green), the user picks one option and the engine handles both gene loci correctly. Two Cobalts paired produces 25% Sky, 50% Cobalt, 25% Mauve, the 1:2:1 incomplete-dominance ratio.

What is the difference between Single Factor (SF) and Double Factor (DF)?

For incompletely dominant mutations like Spangle, Grey, Violet, Yellow Face, and Dominant Pied, Single Factor means one copy of the mutation gene (heterozygous), Double Factor means two copies (homozygous). SF and DF often look visually different. A DF Spangle is nearly all yellow (green base) or white (blue base) and is often confused with Lutino/Albino. A DF Grey is slightly more saturated than SF Grey. A DF Yellow Face shows stronger yellow expression than SF. The calculator outputs SF and DF separately so breeders can plan exhibition pairings precisely. Read the full Spangle Genetics Guide →

How does Violet on Cobalt work, what was Visual Violet?

Violet is an incompletely dominant gene that adds a violet sheen. Its most striking visual effect appears on a Cobalt blue base (one dark factor), historically called "Visual Violet" by exhibition breeders. The calculator now uniformly labels these birds as Violet Cobalt (SF) or DF Violet Cobalt for consistency with other shortcut naming. The genetic mechanism is unchanged, Cobalt + Violet factor (Vv or VV). Violet on Sky Blue gives Violet Sky Blue (a paler lavender wash); Violet on Mauve gives Violet Mauve. This is documented in Terry Martin's 2002 reference. Read the full Dark Factor Genetics Guide →

What is the difference between Recessive Pied and Dominant Pied in budgies?

They are two completely separate genes with different inheritance modes. Recessive Pied (also called Danish Pied or Harlequin) is autosomal recessive, both parents must carry it for chicks to show it visually. It typically produces large irregular yellow or white patches over the body. Dominant Pied (also called Banded Pied or Australian Pied) is autosomal incompletely dominant, a single factor (SF) bird shows a clean band of pied across the chest, while a double factor (DF) bird shows much more pied area. The calculator handles both gene types correctly and shows split (carrier) percentages for the recessive form.

What is Lacewing and how is it inherited in budgerigars?

Lacewing is sex-linked recessive. It is the result of a crossover between the Cinnamon and Ino genes on the Z chromosome, first identified in a UK lutino strain in 1948 by R.J. Watts. The phenotype is a pale yellow body (Yellow Lacewing on green base) or white body (White Lacewing on blue base) with brown wing markings and red eyes. The Belgian/Dutch research coordinator Inte Onsman of MUTAVI Research confirmed in published findings that Lacewing is the recombination product of Cinnamon and Ino on the same Z chromosome rather than a separate allele. In the calculator, select Lacewing directly, the engine handles sex-linked inheritance automatically (cocks can be split, hens cannot).

What is Clearflight Pied and how does it relate to Dark-Eyed Clear?

Clearflight Pied (Continental Clearflight Pied) is an autosomal incompletely dominant mutation at the Pi locus, originating in the Netherlands. It is a separate gene from the Australian Dominant (Banded) Pied. When a budgerigar carries both a Clearflight Pied factor (SF or DF) and a visible Recessive Pied, the emergent Dark-Eyed Clear (DEC) phenotype appears, a pure yellow or white body with normal dark eyes (not red like Lutino/Albino). The calculator automatically detects this combination and labels offspring as Dark-Eyed Clear (SF) or Dark-Eyed Clear (DF) per the international judge specification.

What is Easley Clearbody and how is it different from Texas Clearbody?

Easley Clearbody is autosomal incompletely dominant. It originated in California in 1992 with American breeder Steve Easley. The body colour lightens to a clear yellow (green base) or near-white (blue base) while the head and wing markings retain their dark melanin pattern. The distinction from Texas Clearbody is the inheritance mode: Texas Clearbody is sex-linked recessive (different gene, on the Z chromosome), while Easley Clearbody is autosomal incompletely dominant (different gene, autosomal, with visible SF and DF forms). Both reduce body melanin while preserving wing pattern, but breeders should never confuse them, the pairing predictions are entirely different.

How does the calculator handle Clearwing × Dilute pairings?

Clearwing, Greywing, and Dilute are alleles of the same dil locus (single gene with multiple alleles), not three independent genes. The calculator uses proper allelic series modelling per Wikipedia and breeder consensus: Clearwing is dominant over Dilute, so pairing a visual Clearwing × visual Dilute gives 100% Clearwing offspring (split for Dilute). Clearwing × Greywing produces the hetero-allelic "Fullbody Greywing" phenotype. A visual Dilute can never hide a Clearwing allele, they're on the same locus. Pairing two visual Dilutes always gives 100% visual Dilute offspring (zero Clearwing possible).

Why is Ino sex-linked in budgies but autosomal in lovebirds?

This is one of the most important differences between budgie and lovebird genetics. In budgerigars, the Ino gene sits on the Z sex chromosome, making it sex-linked recessive, so hens can be visual Ino but never split. In peach-faced lovebirds, the Ino gene sits on an autosome, making it autosomal recessive, so both sexes can be split. The visual result (full melanin removal, red eyes) is similar, but the inheritance pattern is fundamentally different. Calculators built for one species cannot be reliably used for the other. Common Questions About Budgerigar Genetics

Frequently Asked Questions

How many chromosomes does a budgerigar have?

Budgerigars have 32 chromosomes total (16 pairs). The Z sex chromosome determines sex-linked mutations. Cocks are ZZ, hens are ZW. This is why sex-linked mutations like Opaline, Cinnamon, Lutino/Ino, Lacewing, Slate, and Texas Clearbody behave differently in cocks versus hens. Hens cannot be "split" (carrier) for these traits because they only have one Z chromosome to inherit the recessive allele.

Which color budgie is best for breeding?

For maximum mutation exploration, breeders commonly work with three base combinations: Light Green (the wild-type base, produces the widest mutation range when paired with other colors), Cobalt (single Dark Factor blue base, excellent for producing rare Cobalt Opaline and Cobalt Cinnamon offspring), and Yellow Face or Goldenface (adds yellow expression to blue series for visually striking chicks). There is no single "best" color, the ideal choice depends on your breeding goal: exhibition line development, rare mutation production, or general aviary variety.

Which is the rarest color of budgies?

The rarest documented budgerigar mutations in 2026 are Manto Negro (Brazilian Black Mantle, developed by Ley H. Silva Filho in 2021, the first fully autosomal dominant budgerigar mutation), Blackface (Netherlands 1992, autosomal recessive), and Blackwing (Venezuela 2002, autosomal recessive). Combining Blackface with Blackwing produces "Double Black", currently the darkest budgerigar phenotype possible. Anthracite and Saddleback are also rare and highly valued by exhibition breeders. Each of these mutations is fully modelled in our calculator.

What determines a budgerigar's color?

A budgerigar's color is determined by the interaction of two primary pigments (eumelanin for black markings and psittacofulvin for yellow) filtered through structural feather coloration. The base color (Green or Blue series) is controlled by a single autosomal recessive gene. Additional mutations layer on top: Dark Factor (creates Cobalt/Mauve from Sky Blue, Dark Green/Olive from Light Green), Grey Factor, Violet Factor, Yellow Face variants, and 20+ other documented mutations. Our calculator predicts every combination based on peer-reviewed inheritance models from Dr. Terry Martin (2002) and MUTAVI Research.

Can hens be split for sex-linked mutations like Opaline?

No. Hens cannot be split (silent carrier) for sex-linked recessive mutations including Opaline, Cinnamon, Lutino/Ino, Lacewing, Slate, and Texas Clearbody. Because hens have the ZW sex chromosome configuration (with only one Z chromosome), if they carry the mutation gene at all, it must be expressed visually. Only cocks (ZZ) can be split for sex-linked traits. This is a fundamental rule of budgerigar genetics that our calculator applies automatically when displaying offspring predictions.

How to Read Your Results

Understanding the Percentages

Each result shows a probability, not a guarantee. These are Mendelian inheritance percentages across a statistically large number of chicks. In any single clutch of 4–6 eggs, the actual outcome will vary.

What "25% Split Cinnamon" Means

If a result shows 25% Split Cinnamon, it means that on average, 1 in every 4 chicks from this pairing will carry the Cinnamon gene without showing it visually. In a single clutch of 4-6 eggs you might get 0, 1, or 2 split chicks, the 25% is a long-run average, not a per-clutch promise. Over many pairings and clutches, the ratio will converge toward 25%.

Cock and Hen Columns

When a sex-linked mutation like Ino, Opaline, Cinnamon, or Slate is involved, the calculator splits results into a cock column and a hen column. This is because sex-linked recessive genes behave differently by sex, hens cannot be splits. Any percentage shown under the hen column for an SL mutation means those chicks will be visual, not split.

Visual vs Split vs SF/DF

Visual means the chick shows the mutation in its feathers. Split means it carries one copy of a recessive gene but looks normal, it can pass the mutation to its own offspring. For incompletely dominant mutations like Dark Factor, Grey, Violet, Spangle, and Yellow Face, Single Factor (SF) and Double Factor (DF) look visually different, DF is typically more saturated or expressed than SF.

Combined Mutations in Results

When both parents carry multiple mutations, the results show every possible combination. A result like "12.5% Cinnamon Cobalt / Opaline" means a chick that visually shows Cinnamon on a Cobalt base and also carries the Opaline gene hidden. These combined outcomes are calculated using independent assortment, each gene pair is calculated separately and then multiplied together for the final probability.
